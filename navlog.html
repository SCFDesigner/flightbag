1 · THE SPEED CHAIN — IAS → CAS → TAS → GS

IAS+ position/instrument error → CAS+ altitude & temp → TAS± wind → GS

IAS → CAS. What the airspeed indicator reads vs. what it should read. Read it off POH Figure 5-1 (table in section 9). On the 152 the error is largest slow — 40 KIAS is really 46 KCAS — and near zero around 60 KIAS.

CAS → TAS. Thinner air means the same dynamic pressure comes from a higher true speed. The rule of thumb is +2% per 1,000 ft, which is deliberately generous — the real figure at light-single altitudes is closer to +1.6% per 1,000 ft, so the rule runs a few knots fast by 8,000 ft. This page uses the exact form:

σ = (1 − 6.87535e-6 × PA)^5.2559 ÷ ((OAT+273.15) ÷ 288.15) TAS = CAS ÷ √σ

For cruise legs the page does not use that formula — it reads KTAS straight out of POH Figure 5-7, which already accounts for altitude and temperature. The formula is used for climb and descent legs.

2 · WIND TRIANGLE — WCA AND GROUND SPEED

Wind direction on a nav log is true and is where the wind is coming from. Winds aloft forecasts are true; tower and ATIS winds are magnetic.

θ = wind dir − true course XW = wind vel × sin θ (+ = from the right) HW = wind vel × cos θ (+ = headwind) WCA = arcsin( XW ÷ TAS ) (+ = crab right) GS = TAS × cos(WCA) − HW

Turn into the wind: wind from the right gives a positive (right) WCA, added to true course. Same answer a whiz wheel gives, and the same thing your ASA calculator's wind function does.

Rough check: a direct headwind subtracts its full value from TAS; a direct crosswind barely changes GS at all.

3 · THE HEADING CHAIN — TC → TH → MH → CH

TC+ WCA → TH+ VAR → MH+ DEV → CH

True Virgins Make Dull Company — True, Variation, Magnetic, Deviation, Compass. Add Whiskey going down: westerly variation and deviation are added.

  • TC — measured off the sectional against a meridian.
  • WCA — from the wind triangle. −Left / +Right.
  • VAR — isogonic line on the chart. −East / +West. "East is least, west is best."
  • DEV — from the compass correction card in the airplane, ± as placarded.

Going the other way (compass → true) you reverse every sign. That is where most people flip a variation and end up 20° off.

4 · ALTITUDES — PA, ISA AND DA

PA = indicated alt + (29.92 − altimeter) × 1000 ISA = 15 − 2 × (PA ÷ 1000) °C DA = PA + 118.8 × (OAT − ISA)

Every POH performance table is entered with pressure altitude, never indicated. Density altitude is shown for situational awareness — it drives what the airplane actually feels like, but the 152 tables are indexed on PA and temperature separately.

ISA deviation (OAT − ISA) picks the column in the cruise table: 20 °C below standard, standard, or 20 °C above. This page interpolates between those columns rather than rounding to the nearest.

5 · CLIMB — POH FIGURE 5-6

The table is cumulative from sea level, so a climb is always a subtraction:

climb time = t(PA at TOC) − t(PA at field) climb fuel = f(PA at TOC) − f(PA at field) climb dist = d(PA at TOC) − d(PA at field)

Then the POH's own notes are applied:

  • + 0.8 gal for engine start, taxi and takeoff — added once, off the top of the tank, not to any leg.
  • + 10% to time, fuel and distance for each 10 °C above standard. No credit is taken below standard; the POH does not offer one.
  • POH climb distances are zero wind. The page also computes a wind-corrected ground distance using average climb TAS through the wind triangle.

A step climb is the same subtraction run again between the two cruise altitudes. Every one of these figures is a box you can type over on the climb card.

6 · CRUISE — POH FIGURE 5-7

Enter with pressure altitude and RPM; read %BHP, KTAS and GPH. The table is published at 2,000 ft steps and at three temperatures. This page interpolates on all three axes — altitude, RPM and ISA deviation — and clamps at the table edges rather than extrapolating.

Where the table shows dashes (top RPM at cold temperatures) that power setting is not available; the page falls back to the highest RPM that has data and says so.

Table conditions: 1670 lb, recommended lean mixture, Lycoming O-235-L2C. KTAS assumes speed fairings, worth about 2 kt — take 2 kt off if the airplane doesn't have them.

7 · DESCENT — THE 3-TO-1 RULE

The 152 POH publishes no descent table, so none of this is handbook data. It is the arithmetic every pilot does in their head:

distance = 3 × (feet to lose ÷ 1000) NM time = feet to lose ÷ descent rate min rate ≈ GS × 5 fpm (3° path)

3 to 1 means three miles for every thousand feet. Losing 3,500 ft takes about 10 NM, so start down 10 NM before you need to be level. It is a 3° path — close to what every airliner and every instrument approach flies, which is why it is worth having in your head.

The reverse form gives the rate: multiply groundspeed by 5. At 90 kt GS that is 450 fpm, which is why 500 fpm is the number everyone actually uses in a 152.

Power does not change in the descent — you trade the altitude for airspeed at cruise RPM. Fuel flow therefore stays at the cruise figure, and that is what this page uses. The descent card shows both the rate-based distance and the 3-to-1 distance so you can see them agree.

8 · HOW TO USE THIS AS A TEACHING SHEET

  1. Fill the givens off the chart and the forecast — checkpoints, route, altitude, TC, leg distance, wind, temperature, variation.
  2. Work the rest by hand on the whiz wheel. Every box takes typing.
  3. The dim figure sitting in an empty box is what the math gives. It disappears the moment you type, so it never does the work for the student.
  4. Press CHECK MY WORK to mark each written answer against the computed one — green stripe agrees, red stripe does not. Each box is checked against the values next to it, so a wrong WCA shows up on the WCA box rather than turning the whole row red.
  5. SOLVE ALL fills every blank; CLEAR ANSWERS wipes the worked columns and leaves the givens, so the same flight can be re-worked from scratch.
  6. ADD PAGE gives another sheet with totals carried forward, the way a second piece of paper would.
