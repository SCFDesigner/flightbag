1 · THE SPEED CHAIN — IAS → CAS → TAS → GS

IAS+ position/instrument error → CAS+ altitude & temp → TAS± wind → GS

IAS → CAS. What the airspeed indicator reads vs. what it should read. Read it off POH Figure 5-1 (table in section 9). On the 152 the error is largest slow — 40 KIAS is really 46 KCAS — and near zero around 60 KIAS.

CAS → TAS. Thinner air means the same dynamic pressure comes from a higher true speed. The rule of thumb is +2% per 1,000 ft, which is deliberately generous — the real figure at light-single altitudes is closer to +1.6% per 1,000 ft, so the rule runs a few knots fast by 8,000 ft. This page uses the exact form:

σ = (1 − 6.87535e-6 × PA)^5.2559 ÷ ((OAT+273.15) ÷ 288.15) TAS = CAS ÷ √σ

For cruise legs the page does not use that formula — it reads KTAS straight out of POH Figure 5-7, which already accounts for altitude and temperature. The formula is used for climb and descent legs.

2 · WIND TRIANGLE — WCA AND GROUND SPEED

Wind direction on a nav log is true and is where the wind is coming from. Winds aloft forecasts are true; tower and ATIS winds are magnetic.

θ = wind dir − true course XW = wind vel × sin θ (+ = from the right) HW = wind vel × cos θ (+ = headwind) WCA = arcsin( XW ÷ TAS ) (+ = crab right) GS = TAS × cos(WCA) − HW

Turn into the wind: wind from the right gives a positive (right) WCA, added to true course. Same answer a whiz wheel gives, and the same thing your ASA calculator's wind function does.

Rough check: a direct headwind subtracts its full value from TAS; a direct crosswind barely changes GS at all.

WHICH WINDS ALOFT LEVEL DO YOU USE?

FB forecasts come at fixed levels — 3,000 / 6,000 / 9,000 / 12,000 / 18,000 ft MSL — and you almost never cruise at one of them. The book answer is to interpolate between the two that bracket you. The practical answer is that it depends how far off a level you are, and the difference is measurable:

With 3,000 = 192° / 13 kt and 6,000 = 210° / 18 kt, at 95 KTAS:

CruisePositionInterpolatedWorst GS error if you just use 3,000
3,50017% up195° / 141.1 kt (½ min over 60 NM)
4,50050% up201° / 163.4 kt (1¾ min over 60 NM)

So at 3,500 ft the 3,000 line is close enough — you are 500 ft off a level and the error is smaller than the forecast's own accuracy. At 4,500 ft you are dead between two levels and interpolating is worth the thirty seconds. The cruise card does it for you and tells you which case you are in, including the worst-case groundspeed the shortcut would cost.

Two things to watch. Interpolate direction the short way round the compass — between 350° and 020° the answer is 005°, not 185°. And FB gives no temperature at 3,000 ft, nor at any level within 2,500 ft of the station elevation, so that box may simply be empty.

IS INTERPOLATING JUST TAKING A PERCENTAGE?

Yes — that is exactly what it is. Work out how far up you are between the two levels and move every figure that same fraction:

fraction = (your alt − lower) ÷ (upper − lower) value = lower + fraction × (upper − lower)

At 8,000 ft between 6,000 and 9,000 you are two thirds of the way, so you take two thirds of each change. At 7,500 ft you are exactly half way, and "just average them" is not a shortcut — it is the right answer, because half is the fraction.

But there is a more accurate method, and here is where it bites. Doing direction and speed separately is what the whiz wheel does, and it quietly assumes the two are independent. They are not — wind is a vector. Take 6,000 at 240° / 30 kt and 9,000 at 300° / 30 kt:

MethodAt 8,000 ft
each number separately280° / 30 kt
as vectors281° / 26 kt

Both levels are 30 knots, so interpolating the speed column can only ever give 30. But two 30-kt winds 60° apart partly cancel when you blend them — the honest answer is 26 kt. Break each wind into its north/south and east/west parts, take the fraction of each, and put them back together.

The physics is on the vector method's side: in a layer with a steady temperature gradient it is the wind vector that changes linearly with height, not the direction and speed separately. That is the thermal wind relationship.

How much does it matter? It scales with how far the wind veers across the layer. Under about 20° of veer the two methods agree within half a knot and it is not worth the trouble. Past about 50° they part company by several knots, and the cruise card will say so and offer the vector answer. Most days you will never see the note.

And keep it all in perspective: the real wind between two forecast levels is not a straight line anyway — shear layers and jets live in there. Every method here is an estimate on top of a forecast.

3 · THE HEADING CHAIN — TC → TH → MH → CH

TC+ WCA → TH+ VAR → MH+ DEV → CH

True Virgins Make Dull Company — True, Variation, Magnetic, Deviation, Compass. Add Whiskey going down: westerly variation and deviation are added.

  • TC — measured off the sectional against a meridian.
  • WCA — from the wind triangle. −Left / +Right.
  • VAR — isogonic line on the chart. −East / +West. "East is least, west is best."
  • DEV — from the compass correction card in the airplane, ± as placarded.

Going the other way (compass → true) you reverse every sign. That is where most people flip a variation and end up 20° off.

4 · ALTITUDES — PA, ISA AND DA

PA = indicated alt + (29.92 − altimeter) × 1000 ISA = 15 − 2 × (PA ÷ 1000) °C DA = PA + 118.8 × (OAT − ISA)

Field elevation is not pressure altitude. Field elevation is a surveyed number off the chart and never changes. Pressure altitude is what the altimeter would read with 29.92 in the window, so it moves with the weather — and the altimeter setting comes off the METAR or ATIS. On a 29.85 day a 600 ft field sits at 670 ft PA; on a 30.42 day the same field is at 100 ft PA. That is why each card here has its own altimeter box next to the METAR: you cannot get pressure altitude without the weather.

Every POH performance table is entered with pressure altitude, never indicated. Density altitude is shown for situational awareness — it drives what the airplane actually feels like, but the 152 tables are indexed on PA and temperature separately.

ISA deviation (OAT − ISA) picks the column in the cruise table: 20 °C below standard, standard, or 20 °C above. This page interpolates between those columns rather than rounding to the nearest.

DO YOU PLAN A TRUE ALTITUDE OR A PRESSURE ALTITUDE?

You plan and fly an indicated altitude. 3,500 ft on the altimeter with the local setting in the window — that is what the VFR hemispheric rule means, what ATC assigns, and what keeps you off other traffic. Pressure altitude is only a lookup key: you convert to it to enter the performance tables, and then you go back to flying the indicated number.

Which setting? Legally you fly the setting of a station within 100 NM (91.121), so en route it changes as you go. For planning, use the one you actually have — departure. This page does exactly that: the departure altimeter converts every cruise level, and each cruise card shows the result.

Are you overthinking it? For cruise, yes — and here is the size of it. Cruising 3,500 ft at 2300 RPM, sweeping the setting across a full inch of mercury (30.42 down to 29.42, which is 1,000 ft of pressure altitude):

SettingPA%BHPKTASGPH
30.423,00064.095.45.21
29.923,50063.195.25.13
29.424,00062.395.05.05

A whole inch moves true airspeed 0.4 kt and fuel flow 0.16 gph. A realistic error of a tenth of an inch moves them by essentially nothing. The climb is even less sensitive, because both ends of the subtraction shift together and it very nearly cancels.

So the setting is a rounding error on this nav log. Where pressure altitude genuinely bites is takeoff and landing distance, and density altitude on a hot day — the numbers that decide whether you clear the trees, not the ones that decide your ETA.

What does move the answer, in order: RPM (2000 vs 2400 RPM is 80 vs 101 kt — a fifth of your groundspeed), wind (tens of knots), and temperature, which barely touches KTAS but swings fuel flow about 9% across 40 °C. Spend your attention there.

5 · CLIMB — POH FIGURE 5-6

The table is cumulative from sea level, so a climb is always a subtraction:

climb time = t(PA at TOC) − t(PA at field) climb fuel = f(PA at TOC) − f(PA at field) climb dist = d(PA at TOC) − d(PA at field)

Then the POH's own notes are applied:

  • + 0.8 gal for engine start, taxi and takeoff — added once, off the top of the tank, not to any leg.
  • + 10% to time, fuel and distance for each 10 °C above standard. No credit is taken below standard; the POH does not offer one.
  • POH climb distances are zero wind. The page also computes a wind-corrected ground distance using average climb TAS through the wind triangle.

Because the table is cumulative, the bottom line is almost never zero — a field at 670 ft PA sits between the sea level and 1,000 ft rows, so you interpolate to get 0.7 min, 0.13 gal and 1.3 NM and subtract that. The climb card shows both lookups and the subtraction so you can check your own reading of the chart against it.

A step climb is the same subtraction run again between the two cruise altitudes. Every one of these figures is a box you can type over on the climb card.

Climb TAS is an average. A climb leg's Altitude box holds the cruise altitude, because that is what you write on the form — but the airplane spends the climb well below it. The TAS this page shows for a climb leg is the average through the segment, taken at its mid altitude, which is a couple of knots slower than the same CAS worked at cruise altitude.

6 · CRUISE — POH FIGURE 5-7

Enter with pressure altitude and RPM; read %BHP, KTAS and GPH. The table is published at 2,000 ft steps and at three temperatures. This page interpolates on all three axes — altitude, RPM and ISA deviation — and clamps at the table edges rather than extrapolating.

Where the table shows dashes (top RPM at cold temperatures) that power setting is not available; the page falls back to the highest RPM that has data and says so.

Table conditions: 1670 lb, recommended lean mixture, Lycoming O-235-L2C. KTAS assumes speed fairings, worth about 2 kt — take 2 kt off if the airplane doesn't have them.

7 · DESCENT — THE 3-TO-1 RULE

The 152 POH publishes no descent table, so none of this is handbook data. It is the arithmetic every pilot does in their head.

Which formula you use depends on what you fix first. It is one triangle — distance, groundspeed, time — entered from different corners:

FIX THE RATE FIX THE DISTANCE (3 to 1) time = alt to lose ÷ rate dist = 3 × (alt to lose ÷ 1000) dist = time × GS time = dist ÷ GS rate = alt to lose ÷ time

An ordinary cruise leg is distance-driven — you measure it off the chart and get time from dist ÷ GS. A descent flown at a chosen rate is time-driven — the altitude and the rate fix the time, and distance falls out of it. Fly it by 3-to-1 instead and it flips back to distance-driven, which is the dist ÷ GS = time you already know.

Worked at 3,500 ft down to an 1,800 ft pattern altitude, 90 kt GS:

by rate: 1700 ÷ 500 fpm = 3.4 min → × 90 ÷ 60 = 5.1 NM by 3 to 1: 1.7 × 3 = 5.1 NM → ÷ 90 × 60 = 3.4 min → needs 499 fpm

They land in the same place here, which is the useful part: 500 fpm at 90 knots groundspeed is very nearly a 3-to-1 path. That is why 500 fpm is the number everyone reaches for in a 152.

Two shortcuts get quoted for the rate, and they are not the same slope — worth knowing so you are not confused when they disagree:

GS × 5 → 2.8° (450 fpm at 90 kt) GS × 5.3 → 3.0° (477 fpm at 90 kt) GS × 5.5 → 3.1° — this is 3-to-1 (499 fpm at 90 kt)

3-to-1 is 1,000 ft per 3 NM, which works out to 3.14°, slightly steeper than the 3° everyone calls it. GS × 5 is the easiest to do in your head and lands a bit shallow. Any of them will put you at pattern altitude on time; pick one and be consistent.

3 to 1 means three miles for every thousand feet. Losing 3,500 ft takes about 10 NM, so start down 10 NM before you need to be level. It is a 3° path — close to what every airliner and every instrument approach flies, which is why it is worth having in your head.

The reverse form gives the rate: multiply groundspeed by 5. At 90 kt GS that is 450 fpm, which is why 500 fpm is the number everyone actually uses in a 152.

Power does not change in the descent — you trade the altitude for airspeed at cruise RPM. Fuel flow therefore stays at the cruise figure, and that is what this page uses.

Can you just reuse the cruise groundspeed? Nearly. Two things pull in opposite directions and mostly cancel: at constant power you pick up indicated airspeed going downhill, but you are descending into denser air, and the same indicated speed is a lower TAS down low. In the worked example above, cruise gives 93 kt GS and the descent 90 kt — a 3 kt difference worth about 0.2 NM over the whole descent. Fine to reuse in your head; the page uses the real one, computed at the mid-point of the descent, and shows you both so you can see the size of it.

The wind is usually different down low too — lighter and backed a little by surface friction — which is a bigger effect than the airspeed one on most days.

8 · HOW TO USE THIS AS A TEACHING SHEET

  1. Fill the givens off the chart and the forecast — checkpoints, route, altitude, TC, leg distance, wind, temperature, variation.
  2. Work the rest by hand on the whiz wheel. Every box takes typing.
  3. The dim figure sitting in an empty box is what the math gives. It disappears the moment you type, so it never does the work for the student.
  4. Press CHECK MY WORK to mark each written answer against the computed one — green stripe agrees, red stripe does not. Each box is checked against the values next to it, so a wrong WCA shows up on the WCA box rather than turning the whole row red.
  5. SOLVE ALL fills every blank; CLEAR ANSWERS wipes the worked columns and leaves the givens, so the same flight can be re-worked from scratch.
  6. ADD PAGE gives another sheet with totals carried forward, the way a second piece of paper would.
